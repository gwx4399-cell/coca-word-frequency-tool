## 1. [Demo] 01 Public Transport

**Writing date:** 2026-09-01

**Task/prompt:** Some people believe that governments should spend more money on public transport than on building new roads. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Essay text:**

In many cities, traffic congestion makes ordinary journeys slow and unpredictable. Some people therefore argue that governments should prioritise public transport over new roads. I largely agree, although a limited amount of road maintenance will always be necessary.

First, reliable buses and trains can serve far more travellers than an extra traffic lane. An important advantage of frequent services is that commuters can plan their day with confidence. If a train arrives every few minutes, a family may decide that it no longer needs a second car. This change can reduce pollution as well as the time lost in queues. Public investment is also important for residents who cannot drive, including teenagers and older people. For them, a timetable that works in the evening is often more valuable than a wider motorway.

Second, transport policy shapes where people can live. Affordable homes are sometimes far from major employers, so workers need connections that do not consume a large share of their income. It is important to link housing developments to stations before people move in. Otherwise, the city may build attractive homes but force residents to purchase cars. Better connections can make access to education and work more equal.

Admittedly, roads are still needed for deliveries, emergency vehicles and rural travel. A significant share of an existing network may also require repairs. Yet building new urban roads can encourage more driving, which eventually fills the added space. In conclusion, governments should maintain existing routes but direct most new investment towards public transport. This approach offers a practical way to improve access while reducing congestion.

## 2. [Demo] 02 Digital Classrooms

**Writing date:** 2026-09-03

**Task/prompt:** Some people think that digital technology should replace most printed materials in schools. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Essay text:**

Schools increasingly use tablets, videos and online exercises. Although these resources can improve a lesson, I do not believe that they should replace most printed materials. Teachers need the freedom to choose the format that supports a particular learning goal.

Digital tools have an important role when they offer something a book cannot. For example, a student learning pronunciation can replay a recording and compare several speakers. A teacher can also use an online quiz to identify which explanation needs repeating. This feedback is useful, but the device is not the source of good teaching. It is important to ask whether the activity helps students understand an idea rather than simply keeping them busy.

Printed pages remain valuable for close reading and extended writing. When students annotate a paragraph on paper, they may notice how an argument develops from one sentence to the next. A book also works without an internet connection or a charged battery. For some families, this is an important matter of equal access. If schools remove printed resources too quickly, learners who share devices at home may struggle to complete homework.

Schools should therefore combine the two formats. They can provide printed texts for sustained reading while using recordings and interactive tasks for clear purposes. Teacher training is essential: staff need time to evaluate digital materials and protect students from distraction. A significant benefit of this balanced approach is that it treats technology as a tool rather than a measure of progress. In conclusion, schools should keep print where it works best and introduce digital resources when they genuinely help learning.

## 3. [Demo] 03 Urban Green Space

**Writing date:** 2026-09-05

**Task/prompt:** Some people believe that cities should protect green spaces even when land is needed for housing. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Essay text:**

Growing cities need homes, and land near jobs is scarce. This has led some people to suggest building on public parks. Others argue that green space should be protected. I believe cities can provide more housing while preserving accessible parks through careful planning.

The case for new housing is strong. When homes are scarce, rent rises and younger workers may have to travel long distances. Apartments close to schools and transport can reduce that pressure. It is important to recognise that an empty site is not automatically a good park; some land is difficult to reach and provides little public benefit. Housing can sometimes be built on underused parking areas or ageing commercial sites instead.

However, existing green areas serve people who have no private garden. A short walk among trees offers space for exercise, social contact and quiet. This is especially important for children and older residents. Parks can also absorb rainwater and provide shade during hot weather. Removing them may create significant health and environmental costs that are not included in a developer's budget.

The most convincing answer is to use land more efficiently. Local authorities can permit moderately taller buildings near reliable transport and require safe, public routes to parks. They should measure whether each neighbourhood has enough open space before approving construction. It is essential that the benefits of development are shared, rather than reserved for people who can buy expensive homes. In conclusion, housing and green space are both necessary. The important task is to design compact neighbourhoods in which new residents can still reach a useful park.

## 4. [Demo] 04 Healthy School Meals

**Writing date:** 2026-09-07

**Task/prompt:** Some people think that schools should be responsible for teaching healthy eating habits. Others say this is mainly a parent's responsibility. Discuss both views and give your opinion.

**Essay text:**

Children make food choices at school and at home, so it is reasonable to ask who should teach them about nutrition. In my view, parents have the primary responsibility, but schools can provide essential support when they turn advice into a consistent daily practice.

Parents influence meals long before children enter a classroom. They decide what to buy, how to cook and whether the family eats together. These routines can teach children to enjoy a range of foods without treating any single snack as a moral failure. It is important that adults explain why they make a choice rather than relying only on rules. A child who understands portions and hunger can apply that knowledge outside the home.

Schools also have responsibilities. Pupils spend much of the day there, and the lunch menu can either support or undermine lessons about food. A practical class might show students how to read a label or plan a low-cost meal. Such activities are essential when families have limited time to discuss nutrition. Schools should also make drinking water easy to obtain, because telling pupils to avoid sugary drinks is less effective if water is unavailable.

Nevertheless, lessons must respect different budgets and cultural traditions. A significant problem with a single ideal menu is that it can make some children feel judged. Schools and families can instead discuss simple, affordable changes and share responsibility for access. It is crucial to assess whether children can use the advice in real life. In conclusion, parents establish daily habits, while schools strengthen them through clear teaching and healthier options. Neither group can succeed entirely alone.

## 5. [Demo] 05 Flexible Working

**Writing date:** 2026-09-09

**Task/prompt:** More employees now work from home for at least part of the week. Do the advantages of this change outweigh the disadvantages?

**Essay text:**

Working from home has become common in many office jobs. I believe its advantages generally outweigh its disadvantages, provided that employers offer clear expectations and keep suitable places for people to meet.

A major benefit is the time saved on travel. A worker who does not commute every day may have more time for family care, sleep or exercise. The reduction in journeys can also ease crowding on trains and roads. For people with disabilities, flexible arrangements may remove barriers that an office has failed to address. It is important, however, to judge employees by their work rather than by the number of hours they appear online.

The disadvantages are real. New employees can find it difficult to ask small questions if their colleagues are distant. Some workers also lack a quiet room or a reliable internet connection. Regular meetings in person may therefore be essential for training and complex discussions. Managers should ensure that remote workers are invited to contribute and that important decisions are recorded for everyone, including those who were not in the room.

A flexible policy can reduce these problems. Teams might meet on agreed days, then allow individual work at home when the task requires concentration. Employers should ask staff what equipment they need and review whether the arrangement affects promotion opportunities. A significant risk is that flexibility becomes available only to senior employees. Fair rules are crucial if the policy is to benefit the whole team. This is an important condition for trust. In conclusion, remote work can make employment more accessible, but its success depends on thoughtful management and equal access.
